## How the loop works

Four roles run each cycle, in order: **Learner** (one angle per cycle — buying
psychology, emotional patterns, persuasion, market cycles through history —
from the worldwide web: books, blogs, descriptive posts), **Scout** (5 new
verified Fresno-area small-business prospects), **Pitcher** (3 draft pitches
for the newest verified leads), **Reviewer** (Gmail watch, rule tests, dollars,
scorecard). Rules are kept or cut only on evidence: a rule is tested only by
sends that actually use it. The cut rule is 10 rule-using sends with no reply
lift. Nothing in this log has contacted anyone unless stated as sent.

Standing rules of the loop: text and email only, no calls. Founder's name
(Mac Lee made it) as principal. Cash App only at agreement time. No fee is
agreed and no outreach goes out until Mac approves the batch and the fee is
agreed in writing first. No invented numbers, no fake urgency, no invented
$Cashtag.

### Kept as a lens (not a pitch rule)

- **Rule 5 — The cycle never changes, only the asset does.** Tulips,
  railroads, real estate, tech: overconfidence, debt, a trigger, then
  collapse. Every crash was preceded by a period where "the old rules no
  longer apply." Average bull ~4–5 years (+~150%), average bear ~1 year
  (−~35%). 1929 took 25 years to recover; 2020 took ~6 months. Mogul rule:
  sell into the fear phase, buy into the panic phase, never believe "this
  time is different." Sources: Vorpp, "A Short History of Financial
  Exuberance"; tzhgal, "Stock Market Cycles History"; The Stock Dork,
  "Historical Patterns in Bull and Bear Markets". Seed run, 2026-11-18.
  Market-timing lens for the Mogul persona; not tested by replies.

- **Rule 17 — Land runs on an 18-year clock — about 14 years up, about 4
  years down — and the two moments that ruin people are the mid-cycle dip
  and the winner's curse surge.** Land prices have peaked roughly every 18
  years for 200 years (1836, 1856, 1872, 1890, 1925, 1972, 1989, 2006)
  because land cannot be made and banks keep lending more against it until
  no productive business can afford it — then credit evaporates and
  everything else pops with it. The dip halfway through (~year 7) looks
  like the end of the cycle and makes people sell too early; the final surge
  (years 12–14) feels like safety because everyone is winning, and that is
  exactly when exposure should shrink, not grow. So: buy the trough when
  everyone is still scared, hold through the dip, and never chase the last
  surge. Treat the clock as a compass, never a calendar — the rhythm is
  real, the year is not. The model places the next cyclical low in the
  2026–2028 window, which — if the rhythm holds — makes the coming years
  the buying window for the Fresno land lane (the four CMX lots: 333 / 433 /
  403 W Church, 2233 S Fruit), not the selling window; do not present the
  window as a prediction, only as the lens saying "near the trough, not
  near the peak." Sources: Progress.org, "The 18-Year Pattern Predicting
  2027's Market Crash" (Hoyt 1933; Harrison 1983/2005; Foldvary 1997;
  Anderson 2008); Willowdale Equity; Property Investments UK; Lifestyle
  Property Group. Learner cycle 9. Land-timing lens for the Mogul persona
  and the wholesaling lane; not a pitch rule, changes no line of the $350
  pitch.

### Untested pitch rules (24)

All of these were extracted from web sources and applied in Pitcher drafts,
but none has been tested by a sent batch yet, because wave 1 was sent before
the rules were extracted and every Pitcher batch is still draft-only.

1. **Sell the feeling first, justify with logic second.** About 95% of
   buying decisions happen below the conscious level (Harvard's Zaltman
   estimate); patients who lost emotional processing could not make even
   simple decisions (Damasio). The emotional verdict lands in milliseconds,
   before price is consciously processed. Open with the feeling (relief: no
   more missed calls, no more lost customers), then the logic ($350, what it
   does). Sources: Eranya Digital, "Buyer Psychology: People Buy on Emotion,
   Pay with Logic"; Neurolaunch, emotional spending / emotional purchase /
   emotional buying. Seed run.
2. **Name the loss, not just the gain.** Loss aversion: the pain of a loss is
   felt roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equal gain. Name what
   the business is losing right now (missed calls, customers going to a
   competitor) before naming what the automation adds. Source: Neurolaunch
   (prospect theory). Seed run.
3. **A small yes first makes the big yes cheaper.** Cialdini's commitment and
   consistency: a small written yes ("want me to show you the demo?") makes
   the later $350 yes easier. Never ask for the money in the first line.
   Sources: Cialdini via Len P. van der Hof, "Cialdini persuasion in
   marketing and sales"; HubSpot, "A Guide to the 6 Principles of
   Persuasion". Seed run.
4. **Scarcity and proof must be real or they are props.** A timer that resets
   on refresh is not scarcity; an invented viewer count is not social proof.
   Real scarcity (a calendar that is actually full, one deal at a time) and
   real proof (a named peer who consented) are the only ones that hold. This
   matches Mac's own rule: no invented numbers. Source: Cialdini via Len P.
   van der Hof. Seed run.
6. **Sadness spends, anxiety hides.** Sadness increases willingness to pay
   (buying restores a sense of agency); anxiety suppresses spending or
   redirects it to safe, familiar brands. A pitch to a stressed owner should
   feel safe and familiar, not exciting and new. Source: Neurolaunch. Seed
   run.
7. **A stressed buyer stops comparing and grabs the first clear, familiar
   answer that ends the decision.** Under stress, working memory shrinks,
   attention tunnels to one thing, and the person wants the open question
   closed more than they want the best answer. One option, one number, one
   easy next step — never a menu, never a long comparison. A stressed brain
   either freezes or picks the cheapest number at the bottom. Source:
   Neurolaunch, "Stress and Decision-Making". Learner cycle 1.
8. **The deadline must live in the buyer's world, never in the seller's.**
   Urgency works because "I must act now" shuts down comparison — but a
   deadline the seller invents ("offer ends Friday") is a prop, and once the
   buyer spots it, trust is gone for good. A deadline the buyer already owns
   is real: every day they wait is another day of missed calls going to the
   competitor across town. Never invent a clock; point at the clock already
   ticking in the buyer's business. Sources: ReadWeave, "Fear, Greed, and
   FOMO"; PsychoTricks on fake urgency. Learner cycle 2.
9. **Once the buyer says yes, stop selling.** Newton bought South Sea shares
   early and sold at a profit, then watched friends who held on grow richer,
   re-entered near the peak, and lost about £20,000. A good decision,
   re-opened by watching other people win, became a ruinous one. The same
   thing happens inside a pitch, in reverse: once the owner says yes to the
   demo, every extra sentence, feature, or reason re-opens a decision that
   was already closed. The close ends at the yes. Source: History Decoded,
   "The South Sea Bubble: How Isaac Newton Lost His Fortune". Learner
   cycle 3.
10. **Feelings lie about timing, so ask for the decision while the buyer is
    calm.** In the emotional cycle the most dangerous moment feels like the
    safest — euphoria feels like safety, denial feels like prudence — and the
    best moment feels like the riskiest. A buyer will never act at the "right"
    moment, because the right moment always feels wrong. Never ask for a big
    decision at a scary moment; ask for one small, free, reversible step (the
    demo) that the owner can say yes to while calm. Source: StockEyes,
    "Market Cycles Explained". Learner cycle 4.
11. **Match the pitch to the mood the buyer is in right now — and never read
    a bad-mood no as a final no.** Mood is a fleeting antecedent state, not a
    trait: the same owner can read the same message as a yes in the morning
    and a no after a long day. In a good mood people decide by feeling and
    say yes fast; in a bad or tired mood they decide by facts, compare, and
    bristle at hype — an upbeat, hyped pitch shown to a stressed owner
    backfires. Stay calm and factual, and treat silence or a no as a mood,
    not a verdict: a polite re-ask at a different time of day is a new
    decision, not pestering. Source: Journalism University, "How Mood and
    Antecedent States Affect Shopping" (Gardner 1985; Gardner & Hill 1988).
    Learner cycle 5.
12. **Give something genuinely useful before you ask for anything — and make
    the gift specific to them.** People feel a deep, automatic urge to return
    a favour, even one they never asked for, and the size of the gift matters
    far less than that it was real and personal. The free demo is the gift,
    and it must be specific to their business (their missed calls, their
    bookings), not a generic freebie — a generic freebie creates irritation,
    a gift that turns out to be a sales pitch in disguise creates resentment.
    Give first, then ask. Source: SUE Behavioural Design, "Reciprocity
    principle at work" (Cialdini, *Influence*; Regan 1971); The Social Cat
    on reciprocity and trust. Learner cycle 6.
13. **Put the buyer's bigger real number before the $350, so the $350 reads
    small.** The first number in the message becomes the yardstick (Tversky
    & Kahneman 1974; Ariely). If $350 is the first and only number the owner
    sees, it is judged on its own and feels like a cost; if it follows the
    real value of one lost customer — one booked appointment, one new
    patient, one job — the brain compares the two and $350 reads as less
    than a single lost sale. The anchor must be the buyer's own real number,
    never an invented "was" price — a fake anchor is a trust-killer under
    rule 4. Source: ConversionStudio, "Price Anchoring Strategy for
    Ecommerce". Learner cycle 6. (Originally filed as a second rule 12;
    renumbered to 13 per the Reviewer cycle 5 flag.)
14. **Sound like a local neighbour, not a vendor — one true, specific
    compliment, then the offer.** People say yes to people they like, and
    they like people who are like them: same town, same small-business life,
    same language. Open with shared ground (Fresno local, small business)
    and one genuine, checkable compliment about *their* business — never
    flattery, never a generic "great business!" — and the compliment must be
    true or it is a lie that kills liking on contact. Sources: Lead
    Alchemists, "Guide to The Liking Principle (Cialdini)" (Cialdini,
    *Influence*; Frenzen & Davis 1990; Burger et al. 2001). Learner cycle 7.
    (A late duplicate Learner cycle 7 entry re-words this rule as "earn a
    little liking in one line before you pitch — a true compliment plus a
    same-side frame"; same rule, counted once.)
15. **Proof only persuades when it comes from someone like the buyer — one
    named peer in their niche beats a big generic crowd.** An uncertain owner
    does not ask "do lots of people do this?"; they ask "does someone like
    me, in a business like mine, do this?" (Hotel towel test: same-room
    message 49.3% reuse vs 44.1% for most-guests vs 35.1% for the plain
    argument.) Use one real, named, consented example from the same niche,
    and until such an example exists, use no proof at all. Never lament the
    problem as common ("most spas miss calls") — naming a bad behaviour as
    normal teaches the owner that missing calls is normal. Source: The Why
    We Project, "What is social proof in marketing?" (Cialdini, *Influence*;
    Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius 2008). Learner cycle 7. (Originally
    filed as a second rule 14; renumbered to 15 per the Reviewer cycle 6
    flag.)
16. **Lead with real, checkable expertise about their exact problem — never
    with a borrowed title — and admit one honest limit.** People hand their
    judgment to whoever looks like the expert, automatically (Milgram 1963:
    65% went to the maximum shock on a lab-coated authority's word; Bickman
    1974: a guard's uniform got 82% compliance vs 36% for a civilian). Prove
    it knows their world *before* asking anything: name their exact loss in
    their own niche language. The proof must be real and checkable — no
    invented title, no borrowed credential — and admitting one true limit
    ("I only do this one thing — one setup, not a whole system") makes the
    rest of the claim more believable, not less. Source: Cognitigence, "The
    Authority Principle: From Milgram to Cialdini, Explained". Learner
    cycle 8. (Two Learner cycle 8 entries word this rule differently — one
    stresses the borrowed title, the other the honest limit and ordering
    expertise before the ask. Same rule, same principle; counted once.)
18. **Name the one real "we" you actually share with the buyer — and only
    that one — then frame the missed call as our shared problem, not their
    problem.** People say yes far more easily to someone they count as one
    of their own, because they stop judging the seller as an outsider and
    start acting as if for themselves. The "we" has to be true: the one Mac
    genuinely shares with a Fresno owner is same city, same small-business
    life — both local businesses serving Fresno customers, both losing money
    to the same missed call. Never claim a shared trade, school, or group
    Mac does not belong to — a false "we" is the most damaging invention of
    all, because once spotted it poisons the trust every other rule depends
    on. The pitch names the shared ground once, in "we" language ("we both
    run local businesses here in Fresno"), and the demo becomes something
    built together, not something sold to them. Source: NEGOCOACH, "The
    Unity Principle Technique" (Cialdini, *Pre-Suasion* 2016; *Influence*
    2021 expanded); PulseRevOps; Len P. van der Hof; Prospeo. Learner
    cycle 10. (Cialdini's seventh principle, unity — distinct from liking,
    rule 14, which is "you're similar to me"; unity is "you are one of
    us.")
19. **Let the buyer own it before they pay for it — frame the demo as theirs,
    running on their calls, so saying no feels like giving something up.**
    People value the same thing about twice as much the moment it feels like
    theirs: in the mug experiment, owners demanded roughly double what
    non-owners would pay for the identical mug. So the demo must not be a
    generic walkthrough to watch — it must be *their* automation, set up on
    *their* missed calls, in possessive language ("your follow-up, running on
    your calls"), so the owner experiences a short period of ownership before
    any money is discussed. Then the $350 is not the price of buying
    something new; it is the price of keeping what is already theirs. The
    ownership must be real — the demo genuinely running on their calls —
    never simulated: a fake "yours" that turns out to be a canned video is a
    trust-killer under rule 4. Source: Lead Alchemists, "Guide to The
    Endowment Effect in Marketing" (Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler 1991; Thaler
    1980). Learner cycle 11.
20. **End the demo on its strongest moment — the buyer remembers the peak and
    the ending, not the whole pitch.** People do not average an experience;
    they remember the most intense moment and how it finished. So the demo is
    designed backwards: its single most vivid moment — a missed call being
    caught and booked live, on the owner's own calls — goes at the end, and
    the demo closes cleanly on the one ask, never trailing off with "let me
    know if you have questions" or extra information tacked on after it. A
    long, careful walkthrough that ends flat is remembered as flat, however
    good the middle was; a short demo that peaks at the end is remembered as
    strong. The peak must be real — a live catch, not a staged screenshot —
    or it is a trust-killer under rule 4. If something goes wrong mid-demo,
    the recovery is the peak: fix it fast, in front of them, and the fix
    becomes what they remember. Sources: B2B Sell, "Why the Peak-End Rule in
    Sales Can Make or Break a Deal" (Kahneman; Kahneman, Fredrickson,
    Schreiber & Redelmeier 1993); MagicWorks IT Solutions, "The Peak-End
    Rule: Why Customers Remember Feelings" (Kahneman & Fredrickson 1993).
    Learner cycle 12. (Two Learner cycle 12 entries word this rule
    differently — one stresses ending on the strongest moment, the other adds
    that a mid-demo recovery can itself be the peak. Same rule, same
    principle; counted once.)
21. **Never let the $350 stand alone — give it a real, worse comparison to
    stand next to.** People do not judge a price on its own; they judge it
    against whatever sits next to it. Add a third option that is clearly worse
    than the one you want them to pick, and preference shifts toward your
    option even though nobody ever buys the decoy — the decoy's only job is
    to make the target look like the obviously sensible choice. So the pitch
    never presents $350 in a vacuum: it names the real, worse alternative the
    owner already lives with — keep missing calls and watching bookings go to
    the competitor, or pay a person to sit by the phone — and next to that,
    $350 reads as the cheap, obvious choice. The comparison must be the
    buyer's actual situation, never an invented package or fake tier — an
    invented decoy breaks rule 4 (real claims only) and rule 7 (one offer,
    one price, one ask). Honest limit recorded in the log: 2014 replication
    studies found the effect fragile outside clean lab settings, so treat
    this as a framing aid, not a guaranteed conversion lift. Source: When
    Notes Fly, "The Decoy Effect" (Huber, Payne & Puto 1982; Ariely,
    *Predictably Irrational*). Learner cycle 13.
22. **Sell the cash result, not the story — a true story with no money in it
    still collapses.** Pets.com had everything a story needs — a mascot
    America loved, a Super Bowl ad, Amazon's backing, $82.5 million raised in
    February 2000 — and was bankrupt 268 days later, because it lost money on
    every single sale: heavy dog food shipped free cannot be saved by scale,
    fame, or a clever idea. The internet story was even true — it really did
    change everything — and the Nasdaq still fell 78% from its March 2000
    peak, because a true story is not a business. So the pitch must never
    lead with how clever the automation is; it leads with the money the owner
    keeps — the booking that stays instead of going to the competitor —
    because the buyer is not buying "automation," they are buying the customer
    who would otherwise have left. If the pitch cannot name the money, it is
    a Pets.com pitch: a good story, no cash result. Sources: Investopedia,
    "Why Pets.com Failed: Lessons From the Dot-Com Bubble"; RiskHub, "The
    Dot-Com Bubble and Burst". Learner cycle 14.
23. **A small yes rewrites who the buyer thinks they are — so make the first
    yes written, free, and theirs.** In Freedman and Fraser's 1966 experiment,
    homeowners who first agreed to a tiny window sign said yes to a huge, ugly
    lawn billboard 76% of the time, against 17% asked cold — not because the
    sign got prettier, but because the small yes quietly turned them into
    "the kind of person who cares about this," and saying no later would have
    meant contradicting who they had just told themselves they were. A written
    yes binds hardest of all: it turns a fleeting intention into a visible
    piece of the buyer's identity. So the pitch's first ask is never money —
    it is a small, free, written reply ("yes, show me the demo"), asked in the
    owner's own words, and once they say it, the follow-up names the identity
    they already chose ("you're the kind of owner who doesn't let a booking
    walk away") and lets their own consistency carry the $350. But the yes
    must be freely chosen and genuinely small — a pressured yes, or a deal
    that gets worse after the yes (the lowball), breaks the trust the whole
    mechanism runs on. Source: Neurolaunch, "Consistency Principle: Shaping
    Behavior and Decision-Making" (Cialdini, *Influence*, via Idea & Digest /
    Books for Agents). Learner cycle 15.
24. **Scarcity only works when it is real, newly shrinking, and the buyer's
    own — never invent a clock or a count.** People do not want a scarce thing
    because it got better; they want it because losing access to it threatens
    their freedom to choose, and they push back against that threat by wanting
    it more. The pull is strongest in three cases: when the thing was plentiful
    a moment ago and is now running out (newly scarce beats always scarce),
    when other people want the same thing (competition beats scarcity alone),
    and when the news itself is exclusive. So the pitch names only the
    scarcity that is already true and already shrinking: Mac takes one deal at
    a time (real capacity, not a countdown), and every missed call is a
    customer who is gone for good — the freedom to win that customer back is
    already lost, and the next one is going the same way. No invented "only 3
    slots left," no fake deadline — a manufactured scarcity is a prop, and
    once the buyer spots it, reactance turns against the seller instead of
    for them. Source: SelfGrowth, "The Psychology of Persuasion (Part 7 of 7
    — Scarcity)" (Cialdini, *Influence*; Brehm, psychological reactance; the
    beef and cookie experiments). Learner cycle 16.
25. **Never ask the buyer to trust a label — let them check the thing itself.**
    In 2008 the whole world bought mortgage bonds on the strength of a AAA
    stamp and never looked inside the bundle; inside were loans that would
    never be repaid, and when that came out, trust did not just dip — it
    froze, because nobody could tell a solvent bank from an insolvent one and
    so nobody lent to anybody. A pitch that asks the owner to believe a claim
    ("trust me, it works") is selling a label, and a label is exactly what a
    careful buyer has learned not to trust. So the pitch never asks for
    belief — it offers the check: the free demo, on the owner's own missed
    calls, where they watch the automation answer and book with their own
    eyes. The buyer verifies the thing itself, in one sitting, instead of
    taking anyone's word for it — and the simpler the thing is to check, the
    faster the yes comes. Source: MASEconomics, "The 2008 Financial Crisis
    Explained". Learner cycle 17. (Pitcher cycle 19 now applies it — "watch it catch one yourself" — still untested until a sent batch replies; application is not a Keep.)
26. **File the $350 in the bucket that is already leaking, not in the new-spending bucket.** The owner does not judge $350 against all their money — they judge it against whichever mental envelope they put it in. Put it in "new software to buy" and it competes with every guarded dollar; put it in "the bookings I am already losing" and it comes out of money that is already walking out the door, so paying it feels like stopping a leak, not starting a spend. The pitch never presents $350 as a new purchase; it presents it as the price of keeping money that is already theirs — one setup, paid out of the calls that would otherwise be lost, never as a fresh budget. No invented dollar value for a booking (rule 4 still holds) and still one offer, one price, one ask (rule 7 still holds). Source: Unseel, "Mental Accounting — Why $100 of Bonus Isn't $100 of Salary" (Thaler 1985/1999). Learner cycle 18. (Pitcher cycle 20 applies it — "paid from the jobs/bookings/loads you're already losing, not out of a fresh budget" — still untested until a sent batch replies.)
