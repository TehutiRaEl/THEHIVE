Okay, imagine you're passing notes to a friend across the classroom. Normally, anyone sitting between you two could grab the note, read it, and see exactly what it says and who it's going to.

A VPN is like putting your note in a locked box before you pass it along. The teacher (that's your internet provider, like Comcast or Spectrum) can see you passed *a* box to someone, but not what's inside it. And instead of it going straight to your friend, it actually goes to a trusted helper first (the VPN company), who unlocks it and delivers it for you — so anyone watching only sees "kid passed a box to the helper," not the final destination or the contents.

So practically, two things happen:
1. It hides what you're doing online from your internet provider, your Wi-Fi network, or anyone snooping on the connection (like on public coffee shop Wi-Fi, which is genuinely risky).
2. It hides your real location, because to any website you visit, it looks like the request came from the VPN helper, not from your actual house.

Now, why the ads are everywhere: it's a genuinely useful product for specific situations — using sketchy public Wi-Fi, wanting to appear in a different country to access region-locked content, or just not wanting your ISP logging every site you visit. It is not some magic invisibility cloak that makes you anonymous everywhere or protects you from viruses or scams. If you're just at home on your own trusted Wi-Fi doing normal browsing, you may genuinely not need one — the ads oversell it a bit because it's an easy, profitable subscription to sell with fear ("hackers are watching you!").

Bottom line: it's a locked tunnel for your internet traffic that hides the contents and your location from whoever's in the middle. Useful in some situations, not a must-have for everyone.
