A VPN is like a private tunnel for your internet traffic. Normally, when you go online, your internet provider can see every website you visit, because your data travels out in the open with your name basically stamped on it. A VPN wraps your data in a locked box first, so your provider only sees that a locked box went by, not what's inside or where it's really headed.

Here's why that matters. First, on public WiFi, like at a coffee shop or airport, other people on the same network can sometimes snoop on unprotected data. A VPN stops that because your data is locked before it leaves your device. Second, websites and advertisers normally see your real location and identity through something called your IP address, which is like your device's home address on the internet. A VPN swaps in a different address, so the website sees the VPN's location instead of yours. That's why you can sometimes make it look like you're browsing from another country.

So why would you actually need one? Mostly for privacy and a bit of security, not for anything magical. If you're on public WiFi a lot, a VPN keeps snoops out. If you don't want your internet provider or advertisers tracking exactly what you do online, a VPN hides that from them. And if you want to access a website or show that only works in another country, a VPN can make that possible.

But a VPN doesn't make you invisible or untouchable online. If you log into your email or a website with your real name, that site still knows it's you, VPN or not. The ads you keep seeing are companies competing for your attention because privacy sells well, not because everyone secretly needs one every day. For most everyday browsing at home, you're already reasonably safe. A VPN becomes genuinely useful in specific moments: public WiFi, avoiding tracking, or reaching region-locked content.
